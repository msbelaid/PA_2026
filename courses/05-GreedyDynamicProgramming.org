* Optimization problems

** What is an optimization problem?
#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\begin{block}{Optimization problem}
Among all the \emph{candidate} solutions that satisfy the \emph{constraints}, find one that minimizes or maximizes an \emph{objective}.
\end{block}
#+END_EXPORT

#+ATTR_LATEX: :align llll :font \small
| Problem     | Candidates            | Constraint                     | Objective           |
|-------------+-----------------------+--------------------------------+---------------------|
| Knapsack    | subsets of objects    | total weight $\leq$ capacity   | max total value     |
| Tour (TSP)  | orders of the clients | visit each client once         | min total distance  |
| Coin change | collections of coins  | total $=$ amount               | min number of coins |

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Three problems, followed through the whole course: greedy first, then dynamic programming.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Until now, the problems of the course had one correct answer: find a key, sort an array. Here many solutions are correct, and we want the best one. Every optimization problem has the same three parts: what a candidate looks like, which candidates are allowed, and the number that measures a candidate. Ask students for other examples: a timetable with the fewest conflicts, the cheapest train journey, the best investment within a budget.}
#+END_EXPORT

** The knapsack problem
A merchant can carry *50 kg*. Each sack of spices is taken *whole* or left.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :align cccc
| Sack | Weight | Value (1000 DA) | Value per kg |
|------+--------+-----------------+--------------|
| A    | 10 kg  |              60 |            6 |
| B    | 20 kg  |             100 |            5 |
| C    | 30 kg  |             120 |            4 |

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Choose the sacks with the *largest total value* that fit in the bag.
- Also: loading a truck, choosing projects within a budget, filling a disk with files.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{This is the 0/1 knapsack: each object is taken (1) or not (0), it cannot be cut. The values are in thousands of DA. Let students find the best choice by hand: A + B + C weighs 60 kg, too much; A + B gives 160, A + C gives 180, B + C gives 220 for exactly 50 kg. The best is B and C. With 3 sacks we can try everything; with 100 sacks, we cannot.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Coin change
Coins: 200, 100, 50, 20, 10 and 5 DA. Give back *290 DA* with the *fewest coins*.

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Each kind of coin can be used *several times*.
- Other coin systems exist: with coins of 1, 3 and 4, pay 6.
- Also: cash machines, vending machines, stamps, packs of products.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Every student knows how to do it: 200 + 50 + 20 + 20, 4 coins. Do not discuss the method yet: the next section is about this reflex. The coins 1, 3, 4 look artificial, but they will show that the reflex can fail. Amounts are multiples of 5 DA.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Brute force: how many candidates?
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align lll
| Problem                 | Candidates                | Brute force          |
|-------------------------+---------------------------+----------------------|
| Knapsack, $n$ objects   | every subset              | $2^n$                |
| Tour, $n$ clients       | every order               | $n!$                 |
| Coin change, amount $a$ | every sequence of coins   | exponential in $a$   |

\pause
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align rrrrr
| $n$ | $2^n$              | time      | $n!$                 | time                 |
|-----+--------------------+-----------+----------------------+----------------------|
|  10 | $10^3$             | 1 @@latex:$\mu$@@s | $3.6 \cdot 10^6$     | 4 ms                 |
|  20 | $10^6$             | 1 ms      | $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$  | 77 years             |
|  30 | $10^9$             | 1 s       | $2.7 \cdot 10^{32}$  | $10^{16}$ years      |
|  60 | $1.2 \cdot 10^{18}$ | 36 years | $8.3 \cdot 10^{81}$  |                      |

\pause
- The three problems are *NP-hard*: no polynomial algorithm is known.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{The times assume one billion candidates per second. For the coins, the recursion that tries every first coin makes 2.3 times 10 to the power 14 calls for 290 DA with the DA coins, about 3 days, although there are only 913 different ways to pay 290 DA. NP-hard does not mean that every instance is hard: we will see that the knapsack and the coins are easy when the capacity or the amount is small. Two ways out: give up the guarantee and be fast (greedy), or be clever and avoid repeated work (dynamic programming).}
#+END_EXPORT

* Greedy algorithms

** What is a greedy algorithm?
#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\begin{block}{Greedy algorithm}
A greedy algorithm builds the solution step by step. At each step, it makes the choice that looks the \emph{best now}, and never changes it.
\end{block}
#+END_EXPORT

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- No exploration, no going back: usually a sort and a loop, so *fast*.
- *Simple* to write.
- But the best local choice does not always lead to the best solution: a greedy algorithm needs a *proof* (or a counterexample).

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Compare with brute force: brute force looks at every candidate, a greedy algorithm looks at only one, built choice by choice. When it works, it is the fastest possible approach. The difficulty is not to write it, but to know whether it is correct. We now apply the idea to the three problems: for each one, what is the "best now" choice?}
#+END_EXPORT

** Greedy knapsack: which choice?
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align lll
| Order                   | Taken                         | Value |
|-------------------------+-------------------------------+-------|
| most valuable first     | C, then B                     |   220 |
| lightest first          | A, B (C no longer fits)       |   160 |
| best value per kg first | A, B (C no longer fits)       |   160 |

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- The best is 220 (B and C): only "most valuable first" finds it, here.
- But it fails too: one sack of 50 kg worth 200, two sacks of 25 kg worth 150 each.
- One counterexample is enough to reject a rule.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Let students propose rules before showing the table. Each rule takes the sacks in its order, and skips a sack that no longer fits. Most valuable first: C (30 kg), then B (20 kg), the bag is full. In the counterexample, it takes the sack worth 200 and the bag is full, while the two small sacks give 300. The weight alone and the value alone are not good criteria: what matters is what each kilogram brings, the value per kg. Is it the right rule?}
#+END_EXPORT

#+begin_src java
// coins in decreasing order, for example {200, 100, 50, 20, 10, 5}
List<Integer> change(int amount, int[] coins) {
    List<Integer> used = new ArrayList<>();
    for (int c : coins)
        while (amount >= c) {           // the largest coin that fits
            amount -= c;
            used.add(c);
        }
    return used;
}
#+end_src

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\uncover<6->{\begin{itemize}\item $O(k + \text{number of coins given})$, with $k$ kinds of coins. 4 coins: optimal.\end{itemize}}
\note{This is what every cashier does, without thinking: at each step, the largest coin that fits. The loop takes as many coins of each value as possible, starting with the largest. With an amount that is not a multiple of the smallest coin, the method stops with a remainder; a real version would check that amount is 0 at the end. The number of coins can also be computed with divisions: amount / c coins of value c, then amount \% c. Ask: is it always the best?}
#+END_EXPORT

** Greedy: fast, but not always optimal
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align llll
| Problem     | Brute force           | Greedy                       | On our example        |
|-------------+-----------------------+------------------------------+-----------------------|
| Knapsack    | $2^n$                 | $O(n \log n)$                | 160 instead of 220    |
| Tour        | $n!$                  | $O(n^2)$                     | 30 km instead of 25   |
| Coin change | exponential in $a$    | $O(k + \text{coins given})$  | DA: optimal           |
|             |                       |                              | 1, 3, 4: 3 coins, not 2 |

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Greedy is optimal only with a *proof*: for example, canonical coins.
- Otherwise: a fast *heuristic*, often good, never guaranteed.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Summarize with the students: on every problem greedy is very fast, polynomial where brute force is exponential. But it is optimal only in special cases, and we must prove it. When we cannot prove it, greedy is still useful: as a first solution, as a bound, or when an approximate answer is enough. When the exact best answer is needed, we need another idea.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Exercise: greedy or not?
1. Coins 1, 10 and 25: pay 30 with the greedy algorithm. Is it the fewest coins?
2. A car drives 300 km on a full tank. There are gas stations at km 150, 280, 350, 500 and 620; the destination is at km 700. Which greedy rule gives the fewest stops? Apply it.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Answers. 1. Greedy: 25 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1, six coins; the best is 10 + 10 + 10, three coins. These were the American coins without the 5 cents. 2. Drive as far as possible: stop at the last station you can reach. From km 0 you reach km 300, so stop at 280; from 280 you reach 580, so stop at 500; from 500 you reach 800, past the destination. Two stops. It is optimal: any other first stop is earlier, so it can only reach less far.}
#+END_EXPORT

* Dynamic programming

** What is dynamic programming?
#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\begin{block}{Dynamic programming}
Solve each subproblem \emph{once}, store its result in a table, and build the solutions of bigger subproblems from the solutions of smaller ones.
\end{block}
#+END_EXPORT

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Like brute force, it tries *every choice*: the answer is exact.
- But the same subproblems come back many times: it solves each one *only once*.
- Two conditions: *optimal substructure* and *overlapping subproblems*.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Optimal substructure: the best solution is built from the best solutions of subproblems (the best way to pay 6, without its first coin, is a best way to pay what remains). Overlapping subproblems: the same subproblems appear many times, otherwise the table is useless and divide and conquer is enough. Richard Bellman chose the name in the 1950s: programming means planning, filling a table, as in linear programming, not writing code; he also said he chose a name that sounded impressive to the people who funded his research. Before the optimization problems, the simplest example: Fibonacci.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Back to Fibonacci
#+begin_src java
long fib(int n) {
    if (n <= 1) return n;
    return fib(n - 1) + fib(n - 2);
}
#+end_src

One per different value.}

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Merge sort: the two halves are *disjoint*, no subproblem is ever solved twice.
- Fibonacci: =f(4)= and =f(3)= both need =f(2)=: the subproblems *overlap*.
- The idea: solve each subproblem *once*, store its result, and reuse it.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{This is the difference with divide and conquer: in merge sort, the left half and the right half have nothing in common, so storing their results would be useless. Here the same small subproblems appear in many branches: f(3) twice, f(2) three times. Solving each subproblem once and storing the result is the idea of dynamic programming.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Memoization: remember the results

#+begin_src java
long fib(int n) {
    long[] f = new long[n + 1];
    f[0] = 0;
    if (n > 0) f[1] = 1;
    for (int i = 2; i <= n; i++)
        f[i] = f[i - 1] + f[i - 2];     // both already in the table
    return f[n];
}
#+end_src

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Instead of starting from n and going down, start from the base cases and go up: when we compute f[i], the two cells it needs are already filled. No recursion, no memo lookups, no stack: a simple loop, O(n) time and O(n) memory. The test n > 0 avoids writing f[1] in an array of size 1.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Two variables are enough
#+begin_src java
long fib(int n) {
    if (n == 0) return 0;
    long prev = 0, cur = 1;            // f(i - 1) and f(i), for i = 1
    for (int i = 2; i <= n; i++) {
        long next = prev + cur;
        prev = cur;
        cur = next;
    }
    return cur;
}
#+end_src

\pause
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align lll
| Version         | Time         | Memory   |
|-----------------+--------------+----------|
| recursive       | $O(1.6^n)$   | $O(n)$   |
| memoization     | $O(n)$       | $O(n)$   |
| table           | $O(n)$       | $O(n)$   |
| two variables   | $O(n)$       | $O(1)$   |

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Each cell of the table only uses the two cells before it: once f[i] is computed, f[i - 2] is never read again. We can keep only the last two values. This is frequent in dynamic programming: the table is a tool to think, and the final code often keeps only one or two rows of it. The memory of the recursive version is the depth of the stack.}
#+END_EXPORT

** The method
#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
1. *Subproblem*: what does $T[i]$ mean?
2. *Recurrence*: $T[i]$ from smaller subproblems.
3. *Base cases*: the subproblems solved directly.
4. *Order*: each cell needs only cells already filled.
5. *Answer*: which cell (or the best of all cells)?

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{For Fibonacci: T[i] is fib(i); T[i] = T[i - 1] + T[i - 2]; T[0] = 0 and T[1] = 1; increasing i; the answer is T[n]. Step 1 is the hard one: once the meaning of a cell is clear, the recurrence is often easy to write. Always write that sentence, in a comment above the table. We now apply the method to the three problems of the course.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Coin change: the code
#+begin_src java
// t[a]: the fewest coins to pay a (INF if a cannot be paid)
int minCoins(int amount, int[] coins) {
    final int INF = Integer.MAX_VALUE;
    int[] t = new int[amount + 1];
    t[0] = 0;
    for (int a = 1; a <= amount; a++) {
        t[a] = INF;
        for (int c : coins)
            if (c <= a && t[a - c] != INF)
                t[a] = Math.min(t[a], t[a - c] + 1);
    }
    return t[amount];
}
#+end_src

\pause
- $O(a \times k)$ time, $O(a)$ memory, for an amount $a$ and $k$ kinds of coins.
- Optimal for *any* coin system, where greedy is optimal only for canonical ones.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{The comment on the first line is step 1 of the method: always write it. The test t[a - c] != INF is needed because some amounts cannot be paid (with the coins 3 and 4 only, 5 cannot), and INF + 1 would overflow into a negative number. For 290 DA with the 6 DA coins: 290 times 6 = 1740 operations, instead of 2.3 times 10 to the power 14 calls for the recursion. Slower than greedy, but always right.}

** Top-down or bottom-up?
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align lll
|          | Top-down (memoization)       | Bottom-up (table)          |
|----------+------------------------------+----------------------------|
| Code     | the recursion, plus a memo   | loops that fill a table    |
| Computes | only the needed subproblems  | every subproblem           |
| Order    | automatic                    | must be chosen             |
| Stack    | depth up to the problem size | none                       |
| Memory   | the whole memo               | can keep only the last row |

\pause
- Same idea, same cost in most cases.
- Start with the recursion (easy to find), add a memo, then turn it into loops if needed.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Top-down is the natural way when the recurrence comes first: the code is the recurrence, and the order is found by the recursion itself. It wins when many subproblems are never needed. Bottom-up wins for large problems: no stack overflow, no hash table, and the possibility to keep only the last values (Fibonacci with two variables). The knapsack follows exactly this path: recursion, then table.}
#+END_EXPORT

#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- Look at the last sack $i$ (weight $w_i$, value $v_i$): skip it, or take it and lose $w_i$ kg of capacity. Keep the best.
- Base case: with no sack, $\text{best}(0, c) = 0$. Answer: $\text{best}(n, \text{capacity})$.
- Example: $\text{best}(3, 50) = \max(160,\ 120 + 100) = 220$: skip or take C.
- Two choices per sack: up to $2^n$ calls.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{This is the same take or skip choice as the recursion of subset sum in course 04, with values instead of true and false: subset sum is the special case where the value of an object is its weight. Unlike greedy, both choices are tried for every sack: the answer is exact. The sack considered is the last of the first i: after the choice, the subproblem only has the first i - 1 sacks, with the same capacity (skip) or c - w kg (take). With A, B, C and 50 kg: skipping C leaves A and B with 50 kg, 160; taking C (120) leaves A and B with 20 kg, 100. Ask: what is best(2, 20)? B alone, 100. Each call makes up to two calls, and the depth is n: up to 2 to the power n calls.}
#+END_EXPORT

** The knapsack as a table

- $T(S, j)$: the shortest path that leaves W, visits *exactly the clients of $S$*, and ends at $j \in S$.
- The client $i$ visited just before $j$:
  \[ T(S, j) = \min_{i \,\in\, S,\ i \neq j} \big( T(S \setminus \{j\}, i) + d(i, j) \big), \qquad T(\{j\}, j) = d(W, j) \]
- Order: by size of $S$. Answer: $\min_j \big( T(\text{all}, j) + d(j, W) \big)$.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{The key observation: once we know the set of clients already visited and where we are, the order in which they were visited no longer matters for the rest of the tour. So we do not need to remember the whole order, only the set and the last client. This is the Held and Karp algorithm (1962). The table has one row per subset of clients, and one column per last client.}

- Same tour as around the circle, in one direction or the other.
- Cells: $2^n \times n$, each in $O(n)$: $O(2^n n^2)$.
- $n = 20$: $4 \cdot 10^8$ operations, instead of $20! \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$ tours.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Size 1: from W directly to the client. Size 2: for example T({A, B}, A): visit B first (4), then go to A (7): 11. Size 3 (the arrows): to end at A after B and C, arrive from B (T({B, C}, B) + d(B, A) = 20 + 7) or from C (14 + 8): 22. The answer 25 is reached twice (22 + 3 and 21 + 4): the two directions of the same tour. Still exponential: the TSP is NP-hard, and nobody knows a polynomial algorithm. But 2 to the power n is far smaller than n factorial: brute force stops around 12 clients, this table goes up to about 25. The next course (branch and bound) goes further in practice.}
#+END_EXPORT

* Solution: the cheapest path

** The cheapest path: the method
#+ATTR_BEAMER: :overlay <+(1)->
- *Subproblem*: $P[i][j]$, the smallest sum of a path from the top left to the cell $(i, j)$.
- *Recurrence*: we arrive from above or from the left:
  \[ P[i][j] = g[i][j] + \min\big(P[i-1][j],\ P[i][j-1]\big) \]
- *Base cases*: $P[0][0] = g[0][0]$; on the first row and the first column, only one choice.
- *Order*: row by row, from left to right. *Answer*: $P[n-1][m-1]$.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Optimal substructure: the best path to (i, j) ends with a best path to the cell above or to the cell on the left. Overlapping subproblems: many paths go through the same cell. On the first row, we can only arrive from the left: P[0][j] = g[0][j] + P[0][j - 1]; on the first column, only from above.}
#+END_EXPORT

** The cheapest path: the code
#+begin_src java
// p[i][j]: the smallest sum of a path from (0, 0) to (i, j)
int cheapestPath(int[][] g) {
    int n = g.length, m = g[0].length;
    int[][] p = new int[n][m];
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j < m; j++) {
            if (i == 0 && j == 0) p[i][j] = g[0][0];
            else if (i == 0) p[i][j] = g[i][j] + p[i][j - 1];      // from the left
            else if (j == 0) p[i][j] = g[i][j] + p[i - 1][j];      // from above
            else p[i][j] = g[i][j] + Math.min(p[i - 1][j], p[i][j - 1]);
        }
    return p[n - 1][m - 1];
}
#+end_src

\pause
- $O(n \times m)$ time, instead of $\binom{n + m - 2}{n - 1}$ paths: $20 \times 20$, 400 cells instead of $3.5 \cdot 10^{10}$ paths.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Each cell is computed once, in constant time. The cells used (above and on the left) are already filled in the row by row order. As for Fibonacci, each row only uses the previous one: one row is enough for the value alone. Variants: count the paths (P[i][j] = P[i - 1][j] + P[i][j - 1]), forbid some cells, allow diagonal moves.}

* Summary

** Key takeaways
- *Optimization problem*: the best candidate among many. Brute force is exponential.
- *Greedy*: one choice per step, never revised. Fast, but optimal only with a proof.
- *Dynamic programming*: try every choice, but solve each subproblem once, in a table. Top-down (memo) or bottom-up (loops).
- The method: subproblem, recurrence, base cases, order, answer. The hard part: the sentence that defines a cell.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Ask students to list the problems of the course and, for each one, the sentence that defines a cell: fib(i); the fewest coins to pay a; the best value with the first i sacks and a capacity c; the shortest path from W through the clients of S ending at j; the cheapest path to the cell (i, j). Most of the work of dynamic programming is finding this sentence.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Three problems, three approaches
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align llll :font \footnotesize
|             | Brute force          | Greedy                        | Dynamic programming     |
|-------------+----------------------+-------------------------------+-------------------------|
| Knapsack    | $O(2^n)$             | $O(n \log n)$, not optimal    | $O(n \times \text{capacity})$ |
| Tour        | $O(n!)$              | $O(n^2)$, not optimal         | $O(2^n n^2)$            |
| Coin change | exponential in $a$   | $O(k + \text{coins})$, canonical coins | $O(a \times k)$ |

\pause
- Greedy: fast, approximate. Dynamic programming: exact, slower, but far faster than brute force.

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{Read the table by columns. Brute force is always exact and always too slow. Greedy is always fast, and exact only in special cases. Dynamic programming is always exact; it is polynomial in the capacity or the amount for the knapsack and the coins, and still exponential for the tour, but 2 to the power n instead of n factorial. The three problems remain NP-hard: dynamic programming does not change that, it pushes the limit further.}
#+END_EXPORT

** Three paradigms
#+ATTR_LATEX: :align llll :font \footnotesize
|               | Divide and conquer   | Greedy                  | Dynamic programming        |
|---------------+----------------------+-------------------------+----------------------------|
| Subproblems   | independent          | one, after the choice   | overlapping                |
| Choices       | none                 | one: the best now       | all, keep the best         |
| Correctness   | by the recursion     | needs a proof           | by the recurrence          |
| Examples      | merge sort           | change (DA coins)       | coins, 0/1 knapsack, tour |

#+BEGIN_EXPORT latex
\note{The three paradigms split a problem into smaller ones; they differ in how many choices they explore. Divide and conquer has no choice to make: the halves are fixed. Greedy makes one choice and commits to it. Dynamic programming tries every choice, but each subproblem is solved once. When greedy works, it is faster than dynamic programming (DA coins: a few operations instead of a times k); when it fails, dynamic programming is the safe solution, if the number of different subproblems is small.}
#+END_EXPORT
